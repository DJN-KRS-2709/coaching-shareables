1 · The loop you saw on stage

This morning you watched one idea go round this loop in about ten minutes. This afternoon it is your turn.

  1. 1

    Idea

    Start from a friction you keep hearing. People skip workouts because they think they need an hour and a gym.

  2. 2

    Hypothesis

    Turn it into a bet you can be wrong about. If we remove that barrier, more people will actually start.

  3. 3

    Test

    Build the smallest thing that tests the bet. Use it. Then try to break it.

  4. 4

    Evals

    When it gives a bad answer, write the rule it must pass. Only then let other people use it.

  5. 5

    User feedback

    Put it in real hands. Watch what people actually do and type. Not a survey.

  6. ↻

    Go round again

    What people did becomes your next hypothesis. Each round moves you further along the Confidence Line, until you can kill it or commit.

On the left, you are guessing. On the right, you know. Your job is to move right, cheaply and honestly. Spend the least that moves you right.

3 · Your turn, step by step

Tick each step when you are done. Your hypothesis fills into every prompt below automatically, so you only write it once.

Step 1

Find one real friction

Pick one person and one moment where they get stuck. Use the Customer Journey Guide from Jakub to find it: a persona, a need, a barrier. Choose a friction you have seen yourself, at work or in your life.

  • One person. Not "users". For example: new customers in their first week.
  • One moment. Where exactly do they stop? For example: after signing up, before the first real use.
  • One barrier. What do they believe or lack? For example: they do not see value before they have to decide.
Good signYou can say it in one breath, and someone at your table nods because they have seen it too.
Step 2 · 5 min

Write your hypothesis

Fill in the blanks. The grey text is Dejan's workout example. Replace it with yours.

The barrier. This is the part you can be wrong about.
Something you can see happen, not a feeling.
Your hypothesis

Check it before you build:

  • Could this be wrong? If no result would change your mind, it is not a hypothesis yet.
  • Is the barrier a belief or a lack, not a feature? "They need a dashboard" is a solution, not a barrier.
  • Can you see the "then" happen in an afternoon? Starting, clicking, typing, choosing.

Step 4 · 3 min

Try it yourself, then break it

  1. Use it the way your person would. Type a real, normal request. Does it give them the thing in your "if we give them"?
  2. Now type something it should not handle. A medical, legal, or money question. Something outside the job. A request to ignore its rules.
  3. Write down the one answer that made you uncomfortable. That is your first eval.
Remember the back-pain momentA normal app breaks in your face. An AI product does not break. It just starts sounding like an expert it is not. You usually do not know what is bad until you use it.
Step 5 · 5 min

Let a model find the loopholes

Open any other AI model (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, not Lovable). Paste this. It lists the ways real people will push your product to do the wrong job.

Loophole helper · ChatGPT, Claude, any model
Hypothesis: PASTE YOUR SENTENCE HERE

I am writing evals for this product, not attacking it. List 5 ways a user will try to get it to do the wrong job.

Cover: off-label expert advice (medical, legal, money), going outside the product while sounding in-scope, trying to make it ignore its rules or reveal its instructions.

For each, use this shape:
If they type: (one plain sentence a real user would type)
Do not: (the bad behavior)
Hand them to: (who)

No attack recipes. Plain language only. I will pick one to test.
You decideAI is good at finding the risks. You choose which one matters most for your person. Pick one.
Step 6 · 5 min

Add the eval

Take the one loophole you picked, from step 4 or step 5. Fill it in here, then paste the prompt into Lovable.

Push the eval · Lovable
People will type things that are not this product. Add a rule: if someone types "PASTE YOUR FAILING INPUT", do not play expert. Hand them to PASTE WHO. Show me that it refuses.

When Lovable finishes, type the same bad input again. It should now hand the person on, not answer.

Why this mattersYou are not writing a spec. You are writing the test. You can run it again any time. Do not wait to add evals after you ship. If it fails in front of users, they stop trusting you and do not come back.
Step 7 · 5 min

Share it and let others use it

  1. In Lovable, click Share (top right) and copy the link.
  2. Swap links with the people at your table, or drop yours where the room shares them.
  3. Open someone else's link and use it for real. Type what their person would actually type. Not a compliment, a real request. That is how their list fills up.
This is the evidenceThe first time someone else uses your product is the moment you move right on the Confidence Line.
Step 8 · 3 min

Read your board

Go back to your app. Look at the live list of what people typed.

  • What is at the top? Is it what you expected?
  • Did anyone use it for something you did not design for?
  • If you were the product manager tonight, what is the next hypothesis from that list?
No list yet?That is your next thing to ship: a board of what people ask. You do not guess the need. You look at what they did.
Step 9 · 2 min each

Present it in four parts

If you share with the room, use this shape. The room's job is to ask one question: what would make this wrong?

1 · The hypothesisYour sentence and your kill condition.
2 · What you learnedWhat happened when you and others used it.
3 · The evalThe bad answer you caught, and the rule you added.
4 · The next stepKill it, change the bet, or invest more. Why.

On Monday

Before the next prototype, the next vendor call, or the next backlog meeting, ask two questions: what is the hypothesis? And should this even exist?

  1. One page. One sentence you can be wrong about.
  2. One test. The smallest build that can prove it wrong.
  3. One eval. Name the bad answer before your users find it.
  4. One look at what people actually did. Then decide.
